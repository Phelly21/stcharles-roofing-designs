How Much Does a New Roof Cost in St. Charles, MO?

National price ranges, clearly labeled, plus the eight things that actually decide what a roof costs on a St. Charles County house.

By St. Charles Roofing Designs · October 8, 2026

Get a Free Quote(636) 242-5111

A new roof in St. Charles costs whatever your roof's size, pitch, layers, decking and material add up to. National cost guides put the average replacement near $9,500, with a range of roughly $5,800 to $46,000. Those are national figures, not our prices. What the published numbers say (and what they don't)

Every price you see online is either a national average or an algorithm's estimate. They are useful for setting expectations, but none of them has seen your roof. Here is what the third-party sources report, labeled exactly as what they are.

The square footage that matters is the roof's, not the house's, and a steep, cut-up roof can have far more of it than the floor plan suggests.

One common mistake is multiplying a per-square-foot figure by the home's living area. Roofers measure in "squares," with one square equal to 100 square feet of roof surface. A 1,500-square-foot ranch with a 4:12 pitch and wide overhangs might carry 18 or 19 squares. The same living area spread over two stories sits under roughly half as much roof, while a 1½-story house with dormers and a steep 12:12 pitch can carry far more surface than its footprint suggests.

The eight factors that set the price

1. Roof size

Material, labor, underlayment and disposal all scale with squares. Size is the single biggest driver, and it is why we measure the roof rather than estimating from the street.

2. Pitch (steepness)

Walkable roofs, around 4:12 to 6:12, go fastest. Steeper roofs need roof jacks, harnesses and more careful staging, and they slow every step. Very low slopes cost money the other way: building code requires double underlayment for shingles between 2:12 and 4:12 and does not allow shingles below 2:12 at all, so a flat porch roof needs a membrane instead.

3. Layers and tear-off

Under the residential code that St. Charles and St. Charles County enforce, you cannot add new shingles over two or more existing layers, or over a water-soaked or deteriorated roof. If your house already has two layers, a full tear-off is required, and removing two layers means twice the debris and disposal. We recommend tear-off on most jobs anyway so the decking can be inspected.

4. Decking condition

Nobody can promise what is under the shingles until they come off. Soft or rotted plywood, delaminated OSB, or wide gaps between old board sheathing all need replacing before new roofing goes on. A good written quote states how replacement decking will be priced, per sheet or per board foot, so the number does not surprise you mid-job.

5. Material

Asphalt shingles are the least expensive per square, with architectural shingles costing more than flat 3-tab and impact-rated lines more still. Metal roofing and stone-coated steel cost substantially more up front but carry far longer expected lives. See the comparison table below.

6. Complexity

Every valley, hip, dormer, skylight, chimney, sidewall and plumbing vent adds cutting, flashing and time. A simple gable roof can cost noticeably less per square than a roof with three dormers and two chimneys, which describes a lot of older homes near Main Street.

7. Access

Where can the dumpster go? Can materials be loaded straight onto the roof? Narrow lots, alley-only access, steep yards, landscaping, and homes raised on piers in river towns like West Alton all add labor and staging.

8. Permits and design review

The City of St. Charles lists replacing roof shingles among projects that generally don't need a building permit, but a material change in a local historic district needs a Certificate of Appropriateness, and floodplain lots may need a floodplain development permit. Review adds time more than money, but it can change which materials are allowed. Our St. Charles County roof permit guide covers each town.

Remember that a Class 4 rating is not the same as hail-proof. What a complete quote should include

The cheapest quote is often the one that leaves things out. Before comparing numbers, make sure each quote covers the same scope:

  • Roof size in squares and the number of layers being removed
  • How replacement decking is priced if rot is found
  • Underlayment type, plus ice and water shield in valleys, around penetrations and on low-slope sections (not required by local code, but good practice)
  • New drip edge at eaves and rakes, which the county's residential code amendments require
  • New flashing at walls, chimneys and vents rather than reused old metal
  • Ventilation: ridge vent, intake vents and how they balance
  • The specific shingle or panel line and color, and which manufacturer warranty applies
  • Cleanup, magnetic nail sweep and disposal

Our quotes are written and itemized after we've been on the roof. We photograph what we find, so you can see the decking, flashing and shingle condition for yourself instead of taking our word for it.

Local factors that move St. Charles prices

Housing age matters. The City of St. Charles has a median build year of 1986, but the tract around our South Fifth Street office has a median of 1945, with about half the homes built before 1940. Older homes in Frenchtown and around Main Street tend to have board sheathing, multiple old layers, brick chimneys and parapet walls, all of which add labor. Newer subdivisions in New Town, where many first roofs from 2004 to 2008 are now reaching replacement age, tend to have plywood or OSB decking and simpler roof lines, though HOA architectural review adds a step.

Weather plays a role too. The National Weather Service documented hail of 3 to 3.75 inches over St. Charles County on March 14, 2024, and 2.75-inch hail near St. Charles on May 26, 2024. After a storm, demand spikes. If insurance is paying, your cost is often your deductible, and many Missouri policies now use a wind and hail deductible of 1% or 2% of the dwelling coverage (Coverage A) amount, so check your declarations page. Missouri law, RSMo 407.725, bars any roofer from offering to pay or rebate that deductible.

Ways to keep the cost reasonable

Fix small leaks early, before they rot decking. Replace before a second layer would be needed on top of a third. Get quotes in the off-season when you can. And choose the material by cost per year of expected life, not only by the upfront number.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average cost of a new roof?

National cost guides put the average roof replacement around $9,500, with a range of roughly $5,800 to $46,000 depending on size, material and complexity, according to Angi. Those are national figures, not local quotes. A St. Charles house can land anywhere in that range, which is why the only reliable number is a written quote based on your actual roof.

Is it cheaper to put new shingles over the old ones?

An overlay can cost less up front because there is no tear-off and disposal, but it is only allowed when the roof has one existing layer that is not water-soaked or deteriorated. Building code prohibits recovering over two or more layers. An overlay also hides the decking, so rot and soft spots go unseen, and it adds weight. Most roofs are better served by a full tear-off.

Why do two roofing quotes for the same house differ so much?

Usually because they are not quoting the same job. One may include full tear-off, ice and water shield in valleys, new drip edge at eaves and rakes, new flashing and ventilation, while another reuses old flashing and skips details. Compare line by line: number of squares, layers removed, decking allowance, underlayment, flashing, ventilation, shingle line and the manufacturer warranty that applies.

Does a permit add to the cost of a roof in St. Charles?

For a typical shingle replacement inside the City of St. Charles, usually not, because replacing roof shingles is on the city's list of projects that generally do not need a permit. Historic district review, floodplain development permits, structural work and some neighboring towns' rules can add steps or time. Our permit guide covers each town, and we never quote permit fees because the offices set them.

How long does a new roof last for the money?

InterNACHI estimates about 20 years for 3-tab asphalt shingles, about 30 for architectural shingles, and 40 to 80 years for metal. Dividing installed cost by expected life gives a rough cost per year. A higher-priced roof that lasts twice as long can cost less per year, though how long you plan to stay in the house matters too.
