Commercial roofing for St. Charles business owners

We're a residential-first roofer, and that shapes how we approach commercial work: straightforward explanations, photos of what we find, and a written scope you can take to a partner, a landlord or a lender. Our commercial work covers the kinds of buildings that make up most of St. Charles: storefronts and mixed-use buildings, small offices, churches, converted houses used as businesses, and the shops and offices that line the commercial corridors of St. Charles and St. Peters.

Commercial roofs fall into two broad groups, and the right approach depends on which one you have.

Low-slope roofs

Most downtown commercial buildings in St. Charles have flat or low-slope roofs behind parapet walls. The city's design guidelines for the Historic Downtown District note that these roofs were historically covered with tar or roll roofing. Today low-slope roofs across the industry are typically covered with a single-ply membrane or a multi-ply system, and they depend on continuous seams, solid flashing at every wall and penetration, and drainage that actually moves water off the roof.

Steep-slope roofs

Many smaller commercial buildings have pitched roofs much like a house: shingles, metal panels or standing seam over decking. These follow the same rules as a residential roof, including the 2:12 minimum slope for asphalt shingles, and they benefit from the same attention to flashing and ventilation we give homes. For these buildings we can also offer metal roofing or architectural shingles.

Signs your commercial roof needs attention

  • Water stains on ceiling tiles, especially near walls, rooftop units or drains.
  • Water standing on the roof 48 hours or more after rain.
  • Blisters, bubbles, open seams, or cracked and alligatored surfacing.
  • Loose, rusted or missing parapet coping, or counterflashing pulling away from brick.
  • Clogged or damaged drains, scuppers, gutters or downspouts.
  • Soft spots underfoot, which often mean wet insulation or deteriorated decking.
  • Hail dents on rooftop units, vents and metal flashing after a storm.
  • Leaks that move around, or that come back after being patched.

The details that decide whether a low-slope roof lasts

A flat roof doesn't shed water the way a steep roof does. It holds water longer and relies on every seam and edge to keep it out. These are the places we pay the most attention to.

Parapet walls, coping and counterflashing

The brick parapets on downtown St. Charles buildings are handsome, but they're also where many leaks start. Water gets in through cracked mortar, failed coping joints and base flashing that has pulled away from the wall. The fix is usually a combination of sound coping on top, membrane or flashing turned up the inside face of the wall, and counterflashing let into the brick to cover the top edge.

Drains and scuppers

Low-slope roofs drain through internal drains, through scuppers cut in the parapet, or to gutters at the edge. Leaves, debris and settling can block all three. When a roof is replaced, it is the right time to correct low spots and make sure water actually reaches the drains.

Penetrations and rooftop equipment

HVAC units, exhaust fans, plumbing vents and skylights all pierce the roof. Each needs a curb or boot and flashing that suits the roof system. In the city's Extended Historic Preservation District, the design guidelines call for rooftop mechanical equipment to be screened from public view, which is worth planning for before new equipment goes up.

Insulation and decking

Wet insulation loses much of its value and can hold water against the deck. Part of looking at a commercial roof is figuring out how far the water has spread, because it decides whether a repair is enough.

Codes, permits and rules for commercial roofs

Commercial roofing in St. Charles falls under a few overlapping sets of rules.

  • Building codes. The City of St. Charles enforces the 2021 International Building Code and International Residential Code. Unincorporated St. Charles County uses the 2021 I-codes with county amendments.
  • Permits. The city lists replacing roof shingles among projects that generally don't need a permit. Structural work, re-decking and changes to the roof system on a commercial building can be different, so call the city's Community Development office at 636-949-3222 before work begins. Other towns set their own rules, and our roof permit guide for St. Charles County covers the differences.
  • Historic review. Exterior changes in the city's historic districts can require a Certificate of Appropriateness from the Landmarks Board. Our historic home roofing page explains how that review works.
  • Floodplains. River-edge properties may need a floodplain development permit. The city notes one may be required, and St. Charles County requires one for reroofing in its regulated floodplain.
  • Missouri law. RSMo 407.725 covers roofers working on commercial property as well as homes, including the ban on paying or rebating deductibles and the five-business-day cancellation right after a written claim denial.
Missouri has no state roofing license

Missouri does not license roofing contractors statewide. That makes it worth asking any contractor for a local address, a written scope, and an explanation of how they'll handle your building's drainage and flashing.

What drives commercial roofing cost

We don't publish prices because commercial roofs vary too much to price without seeing them. The things that move the number most are:

  • Roof area and how many existing layers have to come off.
  • How much insulation is wet and how much decking needs repair.
  • The number of penetrations, curbs and rooftop units.
  • The length and condition of parapet walls, coping and counterflashing.
  • Drainage corrections such as added slope, new drains or scuppers.
  • Access: street-front downtown buildings with no yard can need more planning for staging and debris removal.
  • Historic district requirements that affect visible materials.

Commercial buildings we see around St. Charles

Downtown, along Historic Main Street and the South Main district, most commercial buildings have low-slope roofs hidden behind brick parapets, with the parapet flashing and drainage details that come with them. North Second Street in Frenchtown is the neighborhood's historic commercial spine, lined with shops that had living quarters above, so many of those roofs mix low-slope sections with steep residential roofs.

Farther out, the commercial corridors along Mid Rivers Mall Drive and Route 364 in St. Peters include Mid Rivers Mall, the county's largest shopping center, along with retail and office buildings that commonly have low-slope roofs, and St. Peters has its own building department and code tables, including a 115 mph design wind speed. In Cottleville, Highway N is the main road into Old Town Cottleville, and the city issues its own permits, so call Cottleville City Hall to confirm what a commercial reroof needs.
