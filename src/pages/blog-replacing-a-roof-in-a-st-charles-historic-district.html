In a St. Charles local historic district, replacing worn shingles with matching ones is generally maintenance that needs no approval. Changing the material, profile or appearance, such as asphalt to metal or removing slate, requires a Certificate of Appropriateness from the city's Landmarks Board under City Code §400.1270. Six local districts, 3,000-plus properties

The City of St. Charles has six local historic districts and 26 individual landmarks, covering more than 3,000 properties. The local districts are:

  • South Main Preservation District: about 9 blocks and 105 properties between the alley behind Second Street and the river, from Madison Street south to roughly Boonslick Road
  • Historic Downtown District: about 15 blocks and 85 properties from Third Street to the river, between Clark and Madison Streets
  • Frenchtown Preservation District: about 38 blocks and 350 properties north of Clark Street, roughly from North Fifth, Fourth and Third Streets east toward the Katy Trail
  • Commons Preservation District
  • Landmarks Preservation District
  • Extended Historic Preservation District

Exterior work in these districts is reviewed by the Historic Landmarks Preservation and Architectural Review Board, usually called the Landmarks Board, which meets monthly at City Hall. The board is guided by the Secretary of the Interior's Standards for the treatment of historic properties.

National Register vs. local district

This is the most common source of confusion. The city also contains several National Register of Historic Places districts: the St. Charles Historic District (listed 1970, with boundary increases in 1987, 1991 and 1996), the Frenchtown Historic District (1991), the Midtown Neighborhood Historic District (2014), the Lindenwood Neighborhood Historic District (2016) and the Commons Neighborhood Historic District (2017).

National Register listing recognizes a place's significance. On its own, it generally does not add local design review for a privately funded reroof. Local districts are different: they are created by city ordinance, and the Landmarks Board's review is mandatory. Frenchtown and the Commons each have both a local district and a National Register district, though the boundaries are not necessarily identical. Homes in Midtown and Lindenwood are National Register only, so a reroof there generally doesn't need a Certificate of Appropriateness. "Generally" is the key word: confirm your parcel with the city before you start.

When a Certificate of Appropriateness is required

City Code §400.1270(A) requires a Certificate of Appropriateness (COA) before either of two kinds of work:

  1. Any exterior alteration that requires a building or demolition permit. Structural roof work, such as a new dormer, a pitch change or reframing, likely falls here.
  2. Any exterior alteration that doesn't require a permit but affects a significant exterior architectural feature or the exterior appearance. This is the clause that catches reroofs. Replacing shingles doesn't need a building permit in the city, but changing what the roof looks like still needs a COA.

The board reviews applications within 30 days. Because it meets monthly, timing matters: an application that misses a meeting, or an item that gets tabled, can push the decision back a month.

The question is not whether the roof needs a permit; it is whether the roof will look different when the job is done.

Maintenance vs. change of appearance

The district guidelines draw the line clearly at the ends and leave a gray area in the middle. The Frenchtown and South Main guidelines say maintenance of a property does not need approval from the board unless it will change the exterior appearance. The Commons guidelines say normal maintenance does not require a Certificate of Appropriateness.

Type of workLikely status
Replacing worn asphalt shingles with matching asphalt shingles in a similar colorMaintenance; generally no COA

The city's Building Division page adds one more caveat: even permit-exempt projects may require Landmarks Board approval in the Extended Historic District. If your home is in the Extended district, assume you should ask.

What each district's guidelines favor

The city's design guidelines were written in the late 1990s, and the published copies are scanned documents. The summaries below paraphrase them; get the current guidance from the preservation planner before you commit to a material.

Frenchtown (1999 guidelines)

Preserve original roof forms, and don't alter roof lines on the primary façade. Keep chimneys, retain mansard color patterns, and maintain flashing. Preserve original roof materials when feasible. Acceptable replacements are the documented original material, or asphalt, fiberglass or wood shingles, slate and synthetic slate. Metal is not on that list. Roll roofing visible from the street is not allowed, split shake is considered inappropriate, and sawn wood shingles are fine where they were original on Victorian homes. Skylights don't belong on front slopes without precedent, and bubble or domed skylights are prohibited. Typical historic colors are gray, black and brown. See our Frenchtown roofing page for the parapet walls, galleries and dormered mansards common there.

South Main (1997 guidelines)

Preserve roof forms, angle and orientation, plus brackets, cornices, parapets and bargeboards. Don't remove historic roofing that is in good condition, and replace with similar materials. Low-profile asphalt shingles are described as appropriate replacements for wood shingles. Tile or slate should be replaced with matching material whenever feasible. Gutters and downspouts must be appropriate to the building's style. Historic colors are gray, black and brown.

Historic Downtown

The same approach: replicate color, shadow lines, texture and pattern, and replace tile or slate in kind where feasible. Most downtown commercial buildings have flat roofs behind parapets, historically tarred or roll roofing, so the visible concerns there are usually parapets, coping and flashing rather than the roof surface. Our Historic Main Street roofing page covers those buildings.

Commons (1998 guidelines)

A COA is required before any change in roof line, mansard, parapet or dormers, while normal maintenance needs none. Acceptable roofing includes asphalt, wood, slate, synthetic slate, fiberglass and standing seam metal. Clay tile is appropriate on Spanish Revival and some Prairie and Bungalow homes. Roll roofing is only for roofs of 2:12 or less, and exposed felt is unacceptable. Keep original mansard cladding and colored-slate patterns, keep bubble skylights out of street view, and dormers may not rise above the roof peak.

Extended Historic Preservation District

The Extended district uses general appearance criteria: harmonious colors, and rooftop mechanical equipment screened from public view. Combined with the Building Division's note about permit-exempt work, it is the district where an early call matters most.

A real example: the 2018 metal roof request

Material changes do go before the board. The Landmarks Board's June 18, 2018 agenda included a request to install a new metal roof on a house on North Seventh Street in the Extended district. The item had been tabled at the May meeting and carried over. Whatever the outcome, the lesson is about timing: a material change can take more than one meeting, so it shouldn't start when a roof is already leaking.

How to approach the preservation planner

The city's Historic Preservation office is the place to start, and the preservation line is 636-949-3228. You can also find contact details on the city's Historic Preservation page. A productive first call or email includes:

  • The property address and which district you believe it's in
  • Current photos of every roof slope visible from the street
  • What the roof is now (material, color, any slate or tile, chimneys, dormers)
  • What you want to install, with the product name, profile and color
  • Why: condition of the existing roof, leaks, storm damage

Ask directly whether the planner considers your plan maintenance or a change of appearance, and if it's a change, what the board will want to see in the application and when the next deadline is.

Practical steps for a historic district reroof

  1. Confirm your district. Local, National Register or both. This decides whether a COA is even possible.
  2. Document the existing roof. We photograph every slope, the flashing, chimneys and decking where visible, so you have dated photos for the city and, if a storm caused the damage, for your insurer.
  3. Choose a material the guidelines already list. Matching in kind is the fastest path. If you want something different, pick an option the district's guidelines name as acceptable.
  4. Talk to the preservation planner before ordering. Get a read on maintenance vs. COA.
  5. If a COA is needed, apply early. Allow for the 30-day review and monthly meeting schedule, plus the chance of tabling.
  6. Protect the house in the meantime. Temporary roof repairs can keep water out while review runs.
  7. Build it right. Parapet coping and counterflashing into brick, chimney flashing, and style-appropriate gutters matter as much as the roofing itself.
Watch for asbestos-cement shingles

The Midtown National Register nomination records asbestos-cement shingles among the neighborhood's roof materials. If your older home has them, removal has to follow abatement rules, which affects both cost and schedule.
