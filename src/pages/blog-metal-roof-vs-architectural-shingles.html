The short version

Architectural shingles are the default roof in St. Charles County for good reason: they are affordable, every roofer knows how to install them, they come in impact-rated versions, and the Frenchtown, South Main and Commons design guidelines all accept asphalt shingles. Metal is the long-term play. It costs more on day one, but it can outlast two shingle roofs, it handles low-slope sections that shingles can't, and it sheds hail without cracking. The right choice usually comes down to how long you'll own the house, what your roof looks like, and where the house sits.

Lifespan and long-term cost

InterNACHI estimates architectural asphalt shingles last about 30 years and metal roofs roughly 40 to 80 years. That gap is the whole financial case for metal. If you're 40 and plan to stay in the house, a metal roof installed now might be the last roof you buy. Architectural shingles over the same stretch would likely mean a second full tear-off and replacement.

The math flips if you're moving soon. National cost guides put a standing seam roof at roughly double the national average asphalt replacement, and buyers rarely pay dollar-for-dollar for remaining roof life. For a five- to ten-year horizon, a well-installed architectural shingle roof is usually the better use of money. Our breakdown of what a new roof costs in St. Charles walks through the factors that move either number.

The real question isn't which roof is better; it's how many years you need the roof to last.

Hail: the St. Charles County factor

Hail is the reason this comparison comes up so often here. State Farm reported that Missouri ranked second in the nation for its hail claims in 2025, behind only Texas. Locally, the National Weather Service documented hail of 3 to 3.75 inches from supercells over St. Charles County on March 14, 2024, with 3-inch hail photographed in O'Fallon. On May 26, 2024, the NWS recorded hail up to 2.75 inches near St. Charles and 2 to 2.5 inches elsewhere in the county. Our St. Charles County hail history collects more of these events.

How shingles fail in hail

Hail bruises asphalt shingles, cracks the mat, and knocks off the granules that protect the asphalt from sunlight. Impact-resistant shingles rated UL 2218 Class 4 survived a 2-inch steel ball dropped 20 feet in testing, and some insurers offer discounts for Class 3 or Class 4 roofs. But in 2025 the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety tested 24 impact-resistant shingle products, representing about 95% of the market; most rated "Good" and none rated "Excellent." Class 4 is better, not hail-proof. Our Class 4 vs. Class 3 shingle guide explains the ratings.

How metal fails in hail

Metal doesn't crack or shed granules. Its weakness is denting. Moderate hail may leave small dimples that are cosmetic; stones of 2.5 inches and up, like those in the 2024 storms, can leave visible dents in thinner panels and wide flat pans. Heavier gauges and ribbed profiles hide dents better. A dented metal panel usually keeps shedding water, while a fractured shingle can start a leak, but cosmetic dents can still matter at resale and to your insurer. Some policies treat cosmetic damage to metal roofs differently, so read your declarations page and policy language before you choose.

If hail resistance with a traditional look is the priority, stone-coated steel sits between the two: steel panels with a granule finish pressed into shingle, shake or tile profiles. DECRA's published warranty covers hail up to 2.5 inches. That is a strong threshold, but it is smaller than the largest stones the NWS documented over the county in March 2024.

Standing seam vs. exposed-fastener metal

"Metal roof" covers two very different products. Standing seam panels lock together at raised seams and are held by concealed clips, which let the metal expand and contract with Missouri's temperature swings without loosening fasteners. Exposed-fastener panels are screwed through the face with rubber-washered screws. They cost less, but the screws and washers need periodic inspection and replacement to stay watertight. On a house, the fair comparison against architectural shingles is standing seam. Exposed-fastener panels make more sense on barns, sheds and shops.

Code and installation details that apply to both

  • Wind. The City of St. Charles and unincorporated St. Charles County design for 107 mph; St. Peters designs for 115 mph. Shingles need the right wind class and nailing pattern; metal needs the manufacturer's clip and fastener spacing.
  • Layers. The IRC bans a new roof over two or more existing layers or over a deteriorated roof. Either material may mean a full tear-off.
  • Ice barrier. Neither the city nor the county requires it, but we recommend self-adhered membrane in valleys, at penetrations and on low-slope sections under either material.
  • Drip edge. County amendments require drip edge at eaves and rakes on shingle roofs; metal uses matching edge trim for the same job.
  • Low slopes. Shingles need at least 2:12. A shallow porch or rear addition is often where metal earns its place even on a shingled house.

Historic districts and permits in St. Charles

This is where St. Charles differs from most suburbs. The city has six local historic districts, and changing a roof's material generally needs a Certificate of Appropriateness from the Landmarks Board, because it changes the exterior appearance. The design guidelines don't treat metal the same way everywhere. Frenchtown's guidelines list documented original materials, asphalt, fiberglass or wood shingles, slate and synthetic slate as acceptable replacements, and metal is not on the list. The Commons guidelines include standing seam metal among acceptable roofing. The board does see these requests: a June 2018 Landmarks Board agenda included a request to install a new metal roof on a house on N. Seventh St., an item tabled the month before.

So in Frenchtown, architectural shingles in a historic color like gray, black or brown are the straightforward path, and metal means a conversation with the preservation planner first. In the National Register neighborhoods of Midtown and Lindenwood, which are not among the six local districts, a reroof generally doesn't trigger local review, but it's worth confirming. Our guide to replacing a roof in a St. Charles historic district covers the process.

Permits matter too. The City of St. Charles lists replacing roof shingles as a project that generally doesn't need a permit, but a switch to a different material is a different job, so call Community Development at 636-949-3222. In St. Peters, the city's re-roofing requirements say a permit is needed when the roof covering changes to a different material; confirm with the St. Peters Building Department before work starts. Which roof fits which house

Architectural shingles usually win when

  • You plan to sell within roughly ten years.
  • The roof is cut up with many valleys, dormers and hips, which raise metal costs sharply.
  • The house is in Frenchtown or another district whose guidelines favor shingles.
  • Budget is the main constraint.

Metal usually wins when

  • You plan to stay for decades.
  • The roof is a simple gable or has low-slope sections.
  • You've already replaced shingles after hail and want fewer replacements ahead.
  • It's a barn, shop or outbuilding, common in the rural north county around Orchard Farm.
