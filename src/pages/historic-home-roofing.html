Why historic roofs in St. Charles need a different approach

St. Charles has a lot of history on its rooftops. The city has six local historic districts and 26 individually designated landmarks, covering more than 3,000 properties, and the census counts about 2,192 homes in the city built in 1939 or earlier. In the census tract around our office on South Fifth Street, the median home was built in 1945 and about half the homes predate 1940.

Those houses weren't built like a 1990s subdivision. Many have brick parapet end walls, joined chimneys, dormered mansards, galleries and porch roofs tied into the main roof, and plank decking that has been reroofed several times. On top of the construction, there's a layer of review that newer neighborhoods don't have. Getting a historic roof right means getting both the building and the approval right.

On a historic house, the roof that passes review and the roof that keeps water out have to be the same roof.

Local districts versus the National Register

Two different kinds of historic designation exist in St. Charles, and they work differently for a homeowner.

The six local districts are created by the city, and exterior work in them is reviewed by the Historic Landmarks Preservation and Architectural Review Board, usually called the Landmarks Board. The National Register of Historic Places districts are federal listings. The city has several: the St. Charles Historic District (listed 1970, with later boundary increases), Frenchtown (1991), Midtown Neighborhood (2014), Lindenwood Neighborhood (2016) and Commons Neighborhood (2017). A National Register listing alone generally doesn't add local review for a private reroof, but some properties fall inside both kinds of district, so it's worth checking your address.

The Certificate of Appropriateness rule

The rule that matters is City Code Section 400.1270(A). It requires a Certificate of Appropriateness before any exterior alteration that needs a building or demolition permit, and before any exterior alteration that doesn't need a permit but affects a significant exterior architectural feature or the exterior appearance of the building. The board reviews applications within 30 days, guided by the Secretary of the Interior's Standards for the Treatment of Historic Properties.

That second half catches roofs. Replacing roof shingles is on the city's list of projects that generally don't need a building permit, but the city's Building Division page notes that permit-exempt work may still need Landmarks Board approval in the historic districts. In practice:

  • Like-for-like replacement generally doesn't need approval. The Frenchtown and South Main guidelines say maintenance doesn't need board approval unless it changes the exterior appearance, and the Commons guidelines say normal maintenance doesn't require a certificate.
  • A change of material, such as asphalt to metal or removing slate, needs a certificate.
  • A new color or shingle profile is a gray area. Call the city's preservation line at 636-949-3228 before you commit, or start at the city's Historic Preservation page.

The board does take up roofs. Its June 18, 2018 agenda, for example, included a request to install a new metal roof on a house on North Seventh Street in the Extended district, an item that had been tabled at the May meeting. What each district's guidelines say about roofing

Each local district has its own design guidelines, most written between 1997 and 1999. These summaries are paraphrased; the city's preservation planner has the current text and the final word.

Frenchtown

The Frenchtown guidelines ask owners to preserve original roof forms, leave primary-façade roof lines unaltered, retain chimneys and mansard color patterns, and keep original roofing materials when feasible. Appropriate replacements are the documented original material, or asphalt, fiberglass or wood shingles, slate and synthetic slate. Metal is not on that list. Roll roofing visible from the street and split shakes are considered inappropriate, though sawn wood shingles are fine where they were original on Victorian houses. Skylights shouldn't go on front slopes without precedent, bubble or domed skylights are prohibited, and solar collectors shouldn't be visible from the street.

South Main and Historic Downtown

The South Main guidelines call for preserving roof form, angle and orientation along with brackets, cornices, parapets and bargeboards; not removing historic roofing that's in good condition; and replacing with similar materials. They describe low-profile asphalt shingles as appropriate replacements for wood shingles, favor historic grays, blacks and browns, call for tile or slate to be replaced with matching material whenever feasible, and require gutters and downspouts appropriate to the style. Historic Downtown follows the same approach, asking that replacements replicate color, shadow lines, texture and pattern.

The Commons

The Commons guidelines require a certificate before any change to roof line, mansard, parapet or dormers. They allow asphalt, wood, slate, synthetic slate, fiberglass and standing seam metal, consider clay tile appropriate on Spanish Revival and some Prairie and Bungalow homes, and limit roll roofing to roofs of 2:12 or flatter. Exposed felt is unacceptable, original mansard cladding and colored slate patterns should be kept, bubble skylights shouldn't be visible from the street, and dormers may not rise above the roof peak.

Extended Historic Preservation District

The Extended district uses general appearance criteria: harmonious colors, and rooftop mechanical equipment screened from public view.

Signs a historic roof needs attention

  • Water staining at the base of chimneys or along brick parapet walls.
  • Loose or cracked parapet coping, or counterflashing pulling out of the mortar.
  • Slipped, cracked or missing slates on a mansard or tower roof.
  • Leaks where a porch or gallery roof meets the main wall.
  • Curling, brittle asphalt shingles, often a second or third layer on an old house.
  • Sagging between rafters, or soft spots that point to rotted plank decking.
  • Rusted or open seams on old standing-seam or pressed-metal roofs.

How we approach a historic roof

  1. We look before we quote. We check the roof, the chimneys, parapets and flashing, and the attic where we can, and we note what the original material appears to be. A careful roof inspection is especially worthwhile on a house that has been reroofed several times.
  2. We photograph what we find. Photos of the existing roof help you and the preservation planner talk about what's there now.
  3. We tell you whether a repair will do. The guidelines favor keeping historic roofing in good condition, and often that's also the cheaper answer. A targeted roof repair at a chimney or parapet can stop the leak without touching the rest of the roof.

  5. We build to code and the manufacturer's instructions. That includes new decking where boards have rotted, drip edge at eaves and rakes, and flashing worked carefully into old brick.
  6. We clean up and walk you through it. Old roofing and debris leave with us, and we go over the finished roof with you.

Materials and details that matter on old St. Charles houses

Choosing a roofing material

We install architectural shingles, traditional fiberglass 3-tab shingles, metal roofing and stone-coated steel. For most historic districts, a low-profile asphalt shingle in gray, black or brown is the simplest path. Metal and stone-coated steel are worth discussing in the Commons, where standing seam is listed, but they are steel products, and in Frenchtown, where metal isn't on the list, they would be a hard case to make. When a roof calls for slate or wood repair in kind, we'll tell you so honestly rather than talk you into something the board won't approve.

Parapets, chimneys and mansards

Federal-style side-gabled houses in Frenchtown often have parapet end walls and joined chimneys. Both have to stay, and both need sound counterflashing let into the brick, not caulked to its face. Mansard roofs on Second Empire houses frequently carry patterned slate that the Frenchtown and Commons guidelines ask owners to keep, so work on those roofs focuses on the flat or low-slope deck above the mansard and the flashing where the two meet.

Porch and gallery roofs

Low porch and gallery roofs are common on Creole cottages and two-family brick houses. Under the residential code, asphalt shingles need a slope of at least 2:12, with double underlayment up to 4:12. Flatter roofs need a low-slope covering, and the Commons guidelines allow roll roofing only at 2:12 or flatter.

Asbestos-cement shingles

The Midtown National Register nomination records asbestos-cement shingles in that neighborhood, alongside slate, standing-seam metal, pressed-metal shingles and terra cotta ridge tile. Asbestos-cement shingles should be tested and handled under asbestos removal and disposal rules before any work disturbs them.

Code basics still apply

The City of St. Charles enforces the 2021 International Residential Code. If a house already has two layers of roofing, the code doesn't allow a third, so the roof has to be torn off. The city's design table sets weathering at "Severe" and design wind at 107 mph, and while ice barrier isn't required, we recommend self-adhered membrane in valleys, along chimneys and at parapet walls on every historic job.

What affects the cost of a historic reroof

  • How many layers come off and how much plank decking has rotted.
  • The number of chimneys, parapets, dormers and porch roofs to flash.
  • Roof pitch and height, including tall two-and-a-half-story houses.
  • Material choice, and whether the guidelines point toward matching a historic material.
  • Asbestos testing and handling, if older shingles are present.
  • Floodplain permits for river-side lots.

Historic neighborhoods we work in

Our office is a few blocks from the historic core, and most of our historic work is close by. In Frenchtown, the local district covers about 350 properties of brick and frame houses developed from roughly 1830 to 1940, and FEMA's 2016 map changes put many residents newly in the floodplain, so some lots may need a floodplain development permit as well. Along Historic Main Street and South Main, homes mix with commercial buildings that have flat roofs behind parapets, which our commercial roofing page covers.

West of downtown, the Midtown and Lindenwood neighborhoods hold Queen Anne, Folk Victorian, Colonial Revival, Tudor and Craftsman houses with steep roofs and wide overhangs. Even outside the historic districts, design review can apply: in New Town at St. Charles, the neighborhood's architectural review lists roofing, and no work may begin until written approval is received.
