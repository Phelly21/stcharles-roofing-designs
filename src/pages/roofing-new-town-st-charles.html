About New Town at St. Charles

New Town is a traditional neighborhood development inside the City of St. Charles, reached from Highway 370. It was planned by the town-planning firm Duany Plater-Zyberk & Co. after a 2003 design charrette and developed by Whittaker Homes, with groundbreaking in October 2003. The plan covers roughly 747 acres assembled from six farms and calls for about 5,700 homes, laid out around some 90 acres of lakes and canals that double as the neighborhood's stormwater system.

It caught on fast. New Town was named the top-selling development in a 16-state survey in 2005–06, which is exactly why so many of its roofs are now the same age. The neighborhood is served by the Orchard Farm R-V school district, which also covers much of north St. Charles County.

New Town homes and the roofs on them

Walk a few New Town blocks and you'll see most of the neighborhood's building types: cottages, rowhouses, carriage houses over garages, larger single-family homes and live/work buildings with a shop or office below. The styles lean Craftsman and traditional river-town, with fiber-cement siding on many houses. Census figures put the median year built in the area around 2012, and nearly every home dates from 2000 or later.

The roofs follow the architecture. Expect steep traditional gables, front porches with their own roofs tied into the main wall, dormers, and on the attached homes, a single roof line that runs across several owners' units. Most are asphalt shingle, which is why the bulk of our New Town work is architectural shingle replacement.

Roof problems we look for in New Town

The first roofs are reaching replacement age

Homes built in the first wave, roughly 2004 through 2008, are now on roofs about 17 to 22 years old. InterNACHI estimates a typical life of about 30 years for architectural shingles and about 20 for 3-tab, and builder-grade shingles exposed to St. Charles County's freeze-thaw winters and summer heat don't always reach the top of that range. Common signs are granules collecting in gutters, shingle edges that have started to curl, and brittle tabs that crack when lifted.

Rowhouse and party-wall tie-ins

On attached homes, your roof and your neighbor's are often one continuous surface. That is fine until one owner replaces and the other doesn't. The new roofing has to tie into the old at the property line so that water sheds across the joint instead of into it, and any flashing at the party line needs replacing, not reusing. Color is the other issue: a brand-new shingle next to an 18-year-old weathered one will look different even in the same color name.

Porch roofs, sidewalls and fiber-cement siding

Front porches are a signature of New Town's design, and every one is a place where a lower roof meets a wall. Those joints depend on step flashing and a kick-out flashing that steers water into the gutter rather than behind the siding. When that detail is missing or has failed, water stains show up on porch ceilings or the siding at the bottom of the roof line. A targeted roof repair usually fixes it if the decking underneath is still sound.

Pipe boots and vents

Rubber pipe-boot collars tend to crack long before the shingles around them are worn out. On roofs approaching 20 years, they are among the most common leak sources we find, and among the cheapest to fix.

NTGA architectural review and city permits

New Town's homeowners association, the New Town at St. Charles General Assembly (NTGA), uses an Architectural Review Form, and roofing is one of the project types it lists. The form asks for a plot plan, photos, a description of the work and construction detail. The architectural review board can take up to two weeks, and the form is explicit: no work may begin until written approval has been received.

Before you schedule a New Town re-roof

Submit the NTGA form first and wait for written approval. Check the current NTGA standards for any material or color rules. We'll provide the product specifications and color samples you need to fill out the form.

NTGA approval doesn't replace city rules. Because New Town is inside the City of St. Charles, the city's Building Division applies, and it lists replacing roof shingles among the projects that generally do not require a permit. Work that changes the structure, such as replacing rafters, adding a skylight or changing pitch, likely does; call the city's Community Development office at 636-949-3222 if your project goes beyond shingles. The city also notes that a Floodplain Development Permit may be required on floodplain lots, so ask the city if you're unsure about your address. Our roof permit guide for St. Charles County covers the rules town by town.

On the code side, the city enforces the 2021 International Residential Code with a 107 mph design wind speed and a "Severe" weathering rating. Ice barrier underlayment is not required by the city's code tables, but we still install it in valleys and around penetrations, along with drip edge at the eaves and rakes.

Weather that reaches New Town roofs

New Town's open lakes and wide streets don't do much to slow a storm. According to the National Weather Service, supercells over St. Charles County on March 14, 2024 produced hail of 3 to 3.75 inches, and on May 26, 2024 the largest hail reported was 2.75 inches near Saint Charles. In November 2024 the Weather Service confirmed an EF-0 tornado along a six-mile track in northwestern St. Charles County that caused minor roof damage to homes. After a hail or wind event, our storm and hail damage service starts with dated photos you can share with your insurer.

Roofing services New Town homeowners ask for

Most calls from New Town fall into a few categories:

  • First full replacement. For a 2004–2008 home, a roof replacement means tearing off the original builder shingles, replacing any soft decking, and rebuilding the details that age first: valleys, sidewall flashing and vents.
  • Shingle upgrades. Many owners move from builder-grade to a heavier architectural shingle, sometimes with a UL 2218 Class 4 impact rating, which some insurers recognize with a discount.
  • Leak repairs on porches and dormers. Localized repairs at porch tie-ins, pipe boots and dormer walls are often all a younger roof needs.
  • A second opinion. If you're buying or selling, or a neighbor just re-roofed, a roof inspection tells you where your roof stands before you file anything with the NTGA.
