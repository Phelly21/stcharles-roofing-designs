Portage des Sioux at a glance

Portage des Sioux is one of the oldest towns in the region. It was founded in 1799 under Spanish Lieutenant Governor Zenon Trudeau and François Saucier, and in 1815 it gave its name to the Treaties of Portage des Sioux, signed here after the War of 1812. Today it is a city of 335 people (2020 census) covering about 0.68 square miles in ZIP code 63373, sitting on the Mississippi roughly across from Elsah, Illinois.

Most people reach town by driving north out of St. Charles on Highway 94 and turning onto Route J. Students attend the Orchard Farm R-V School District, the same district that serves Orchard Farm and New Town at St. Charles. The best-known landmark is Our Lady of the Rivers Shrine. After the 1951 flood spared the town, residents dedicated a 25-foot fiberglass statue of Mary on a 20-foot pedestal on October 13, 1957. The shrine still hosts the annual Blessing of the Fleet each July, and St. Francis of Assisi Parish serves the town.

Homes and roofs in a river town

Portage des Sioux is not a subdivision town. Houses were built over many decades on small town lots, along with river and marina properties, outbuildings and the occasional farm structure on the edges of town. That means the roofs we look at here vary from one house to the next: asphalt shingles on older frame homes, metal on sheds, garages and some houses, and low-slope sections over porches and additions.

Flood history has also changed the housing stock. Some owners have raised their homes, and some properties have been bought out and cleared. A raised house changes how a roof job runs. The eaves sit higher, ladders and staging need more planning, and debris has to come down safely from greater height. On a house that was lifted, we also check how the roof meets any porches, stair covers or additions built afterward, because those tie-ins are where leaks tend to start.

Roof problems we look for in Portage des Sioux

  • Wind damage on exposed lots. Open river bottoms give wind a long run with nothing to slow it. Lifted or creased shingles along eaves, rakes and ridges are the first thing we check after a storm.
  • Missing or short drip edge. St. Charles County's residential code requires drip edge at both eaves and rakes. Older roofs often have it only at the eaves, or none at all, which lets wind-driven rain wick under the edge and rot the decking.
  • Moisture-damaged decking. Homes that have taken on water, or sat in humid conditions after a flood, can have soft or stained sheathing. We check from the attic where we can and photograph what we find.
  • Tired low-slope sections. Porch and addition roofs built too flat for shingles are a common leak source. Asphalt shingles need at least a 2:12 slope, and low slopes need a different approach.
  • Metal roof fasteners. Exposed-fastener metal on homes and outbuildings relies on screws and rubber washers that wear out over time and should be checked and replaced as needed.

Permits and floodplain rules in Portage des Sioux

This is the part that sets Portage des Sioux apart from most of St. Charles County. In much of the county, replacing a roof generally does not require a building permit. Floodplain rules are different. St. Charles County's floodplain program states that floodplain development permits are required even where a building permit would not be, and it names reroofing a structure as an example. The city of Portage des Sioux has its own floodplain ordinance, and who issues building permits in town is something to confirm with the city.

Before any roof work

Call Portage des Sioux City Hall and ask whether your project needs a floodplain development permit or any other approval. We build the start date around that answer. Our guide to roof permits in St. Charles County compares the rules town by town.

The 50% substantial-damage rule

After a flood, the bigger question is often not the roof itself but the total repair bill. Under floodplain rules, if repairing a substantially damaged building costs 50% or more of its pre-damage market value, the building must be brought into compliance with current floodplain standards. In St. Charles County that means the lowest floor at least one foot above base flood elevation. A roof replacement alone rarely triggers this, but a roof added to drywall, flooring, mechanical and structural repairs can push the total over the line. Ask the city how they count costs before you commit to a combined repair plan.

In a floodplain town, the permit question comes before the shingle question.

Floods, buyouts and storms

The Mississippi defines life here. During the record 1993 flood, the river crested at 38.17 feet at the Grafton gauge on August 1, 1993, and water reached every street in Portage des Sioux. In June 2019 the river rose to about 34.7 feet, streets became impassable and the church basement flooded. St. Charles County's buyout notices list the 1973, 1993, 2011 and 2019 floods.

After 2019, the county launched a voluntary buyout program funded with about $15.7 million in federal HUD disaster recovery money (CDBG-DR). More than 900 homes flooded in that event, and the program is set up to buy up to about 100 of them, with the land kept as permanent open space. It covers unincorporated areas, Portage des Sioux, West Alton and parts of the City of St. Charles, and it is expected to wrap up by early 2027. If you are weighing a buyout, a major roof investment may not make sense yet. If you plan to stay, a roof built for wind and water is worth getting right.

Wind and hail matter too. The National Weather Service documented hail of 3 to 3.75 inches over St. Charles County on March 14, 2024, and 2.75-inch hail near St. Charles on May 26, 2024. On November 4–5, 2024, the NWS confirmed an EF-0 tornado on a 6-mile track in northwestern St. Charles County that partly destroyed a pole barn and caused minor roof damage to homes. Full replacement

When a roof is worn out, or has been through one storm too many, a roof replacement is the better money. We tear off to the decking, replace soft sheathing, install drip edge at eaves and rakes as the county code requires, and add ice and water shield in valleys and around penetrations. That last step isn't required by code in St. Charles County, but it is cheap insurance against wind-driven rain.

Metal roofing

For exposed lots and raised homes, metal roofing is a strong option. It is light, sheds water quickly and holds up to wind when fastened correctly. Standing seam hides its fasteners; exposed-fastener panels cost less but need periodic screw and washer checks.

Wind-rated shingles

If you prefer shingles, architectural shingles carry wind ratings well beyond the basic 3-tab product. We look at the ASTM D7158 or D3161 wind class on the wrapper and match it to your exposure.
