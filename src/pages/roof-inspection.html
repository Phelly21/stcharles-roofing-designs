Why a roof inspection matters here

A roof can look fine from the driveway and still be close to failing. Hail bruises, cracked pipe boots, lifted flashing and soft decking rarely show from the ground, and by the time a stain reaches the ceiling, water has usually been getting in for a while.

St. Charles County is hard on roofs. The county's code table rates our weathering as "Severe." Winters swing above and below freezing, summers bring 35 to 40 days of 90-degree heat, and the National Weather Service counts 40 to 50 thunderstorm days a year in the St. Louis area. Missouri ranked second in the country for State Farm hail claims in 2025, behind only Texas. A regular look at the roof is the cheapest way to keep a small problem small.

When to get your roof inspected

  • After a hail or wind storm, even if you don't see damage from the ground.
  • Once a year, ideally in spring after freeze-thaw season and before the storm months.
  • When your roof turns 15 to 20 years old, to plan a replacement on your schedule instead of after a leak.
  • Before buying or selling a home, so the roof isn't a surprise at closing.
  • When you see a stain, drip or shingle debris, to find the cause, not just the symptom.
  • Before choosing a new roof, to know the layer count, decking condition and ventilation you'll be working with.

What we check

We get on the roof where it's safe to walk and into the attic where we can reach it. Here's what we look at and photograph.

Shingles and roof surface

Curling, cracking, blistering and granule loss on each slope; hail bruises, where impacts knock off granules and can fracture the mat underneath; creased or missing shingles from wind; and how many layers are on the roof. Under IRC R908.3.1.1, a roof with two or more layers has to be torn off before re-roofing, so the layer count affects future costs.

Flashing and penetrations

Step flashing and counterflashing at walls and chimneys, skylight curbs, pipe boots, box vents and exhaust caps. These are where most leaks start. On brick homes, we check whether chimney counterflashing is set into the mortar or just glued to the face with sealant.

Valleys, edges and gutters

Worn or poorly cut valleys, and whether there's drip edge at the eaves and rakes as the county code requires on new roofing work. Rotted fascia and the first row of decking often point to missing drip edge. We also note gutter damage, since dented gutters and downspouts are a common sign of hail.

Decking and structure

Soft or spongy spots underfoot, sagging between rafters, and on older homes with board decking, gaps wide enough to matter for a future re-roof.

Attic and ventilation

Water stains, daylight through the boards, mold or frost on the sheathing, and whether intake and exhaust ventilation are balanced. Poor attic ventilation cooks shingles from below in our summers and feeds ice dams and condensation in winter.

How an inspection works

  1. You call or send the form. Tell us about the roof's age, any leaks, and any recent storms.
  2. We look at the roof in person. On the roof where it's safe, in the attic where we can get in, and around the exterior for gutter, vent and siding clues.
  3. We photograph what we find. Dated photos of each slope and every problem, so you're not taking our word for it.
  4. We explain it plainly. What's normal wear, what needs attention now, and what can wait.
  5. You get a free written quote if work is needed. A roof repair if the roof is sound, or a roof replacement if it's at the end of its life. If it needs nothing, we'll say so.

If your roof doesn't need anything, the most useful thing we can tell you is exactly that.

Hail inspections in St. Charles County

Hail is the main reason people call us for an inspection. The National Weather Service documented supercells over St. Charles County on March 14, 2024 that produced hail of 3 to 3.75 inches, with 3-inch hail photographed in O'Fallon. On May 26, 2024, the largest hail reported was 2.75 inches near St. Charles, with 2 to 2.5-inch hail elsewhere in the county. Back on April 28, 2012, golf-ball to baseball-size hail fell across the county. Our St. Charles County hail history lists the major events.

Hail damage isn't always visible, and it isn't always obvious on newer, impact-rated shingles either. Independent testing by IBHS in 2025 found that even Class 4 shingles aren't hail-proof. That's why we look at every slope and photograph what we find. If the damage is real, our storm and hail damage roof repair page explains what comes next.

Before you sign with a storm chaser

Under Missouri's RSMo 407.725, a roofer may not offer to pay or rebate any part of your deductible, must give you a written notice of your right to cancel before you sign, and must refund your payments within 10 days if you cancel after your insurer denies the claim in writing. See the statute on the Missouri Revisor of Statutes site.

Inspections and insurance

An inspection doesn't decide a claim. That's between you and your insurer. What it does is give you evidence. We document what we find with dated photos you can share with your insurer, and you'll know whether you're looking at storm damage or normal wear before you decide to file.

It also helps to know your policy. Many Missouri policies use a wind and hail deductible of 1% or 2% of dwelling coverage; the Missouri Department of Commerce and Insurance gives the example of a 2% deductible on a $100,000 home being $2,000. The DCI also warns that on an actual cash value policy, a 20-year-old roof may pay as little as 20% of the cost to replace it. Its "Raising the Roof" comparison tool compares how the state's larger insurers cover roofs. Check your declarations page.

What we see on roofs around St. Charles

Historic homes near downtown

In Frenchtown and along Historic Main Street, inspections focus on parapet walls, coping, chimney flashing and the joints where gallery and porch roofs meet the main roof. In Midtown and Lindenwood, we may find slate, pressed-metal shingles or asbestos-cement shingles, which need proper handling if they're ever removed. If you're thinking about a material change on a home in one of the city's local historic districts, our historic home roofing page explains the Certificate of Appropriateness review.

Subdivisions from the 1970s to the 1990s

In St. Peters and Harvester, many homes date from the 1970s, '80s and '90s and are on their second or third roof. We check layer count closely there, since a second layer means a mandatory tear-off next time.

Newer neighborhoods

In New Town at St. Charles, the first roofs from 2004 onward are approaching 20 years old. On younger roofs, inspections usually look for storm damage and builder-grade details like undersized ventilation.

River-bottom and rural properties

Out toward Orchard Farm and the north county, we see open wind exposure, farmhouses, and metal-roofed outbuildings. The National Weather Service confirmed an EF-0 tornado on a 6-mile track in northwestern St. Charles County on Nov. 4–5, 2024, with minor roof damage to homes.
