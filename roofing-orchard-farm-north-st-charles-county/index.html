Orchard Farm and the north county river bottoms

Orchard Farm is an unincorporated community along Highway 94 in St. Charles Township, north of the City of St. Charles. It takes its name from the orchards that once grew here, and it had its own post office from 1894 to 1953. Today the name lives on most visibly in the Orchard Farm R-V School District, with its elementary, middle and senior high schools and a district office on Boschertown Road. The district also takes in New Town at St. Charles to the east, and Portage des Sioux and West Alton to the north.

The land around Orchard Farm is mostly flat, open river bottom between the Missouri and Mississippi rivers. That geography matters to a roof in two ways: there is little to break the wind across open fields, and much of the low ground sits in a mapped floodplain. In 2019, floodwater overtopped the Elm Point levee, which protects about 1,400 mostly agricultural acres in the area.

What's built out here, and what roofs it has

North county is a rural mix, nothing like the subdivisions of St. Peters or Cottleville. A single property may have three or four roofs on it.

Farmhouses

Older farmhouses often carry a history of additions, each with its own pitch and roofline, plus porch roofs at low slopes and sometimes two layers of shingles. Board decking with wide gaps is common under older roofs. Code does not allow a new roof over two or more existing layers, so the next roof on these houses is a full tear-off and a chance to fix the decking.

Acreage homes

Newer homes on acreage tend to be larger ranch and two-story designs with long, wind-exposed eaves and rakes. Drip edge at both, which county code requires, and correct nailing matter more where wind comes in unobstructed across a field.

Pole barns, shops and outbuildings

Pole barns, machine sheds, garages and shops are mostly exposed-fastener metal. These roofs are durable, but their screws and rubber washers need periodic inspection and replacement, and loose or backed-out fasteners are a common source of leaks and wind damage.

Roof problems we see in north county

  • Wind-lifted shingles and panels. Open exposure means more uplift at eaves, rakes and ridges. On metal, panel edges and trim are where wind gets a grip.
  • Worn fastener washers on metal roofs. Rubber washers dry out and crack in the sun, and screws back out as panels expand and contract.
  • Low-slope porch and lean-to roofs. Shingles need at least a 2:12 slope, with double underlayment below 4:12. Porches and lean-to sheds added over the years often fall short and leak.
  • Old decking. Gapped board sheathing on older farmhouses may need new sheathing before shingles can go back on.
  • Hidden storm damage. Tall roofs and long outbuildings make it easy to miss a few missing shingles or a lifted panel until the next rain finds it.

Permits and the floodplain rule

Unincorporated north St. Charles County falls under the county's building rules, which use the 2021 St. Charles County Residential Code. The county lists replacing a roof among projects that generally do not require a building permit. That is where many homeowners stop reading, and that is the mistake.

In the regulated floodplain, reroofing needs a county floodplain development permit even when no building permit is required.

The county's Floodplain Development Permit page says these permits are required even in cases when a building permit would not be, and it gives reroofing a structure as an example. The rule applies in the floodway, floodway fringe and density floodway mapped on the 2016 flood insurance rate maps. Many north county properties fall in those zones, but it's parcel by parcel, so check your address or call County Building and Code Enforcement at 636-949-7345 before work starts.

If a roof is part of a bigger repair after a flood or storm, the county's substantial damage rule can also come into play: when repair costs reach 50% of the structure's pre-damage market value, the building must be brought into compliance, including a lowest floor at least one foot above base flood elevation. Our roof permit guide for St. Charles County covers the details.

Storms in north St. Charles County

According to the National Weather Service, an EF-0 tornado on November 4 to 5, 2024 traveled a six-mile track through northwestern St. Charles County, partly destroying a pole barn and causing minor roof damage to homes. The National Weather Service also documented hail of 3 to 3.75 inches over St. Charles County on March 14, 2024. Our St. Charles County hail history lists the major events by date.

After a storm, we document what we find with dated photos you can share with your insurer, on the house and on outbuildings. Our storm and hail damage repair page explains what we look for.

Services north county property owners ask for

Metal roofing for homes and outbuildings

Metal roofing is a natural fit out here. Exposed-fastener panels cover barns and shops economically, and standing seam, with concealed clips that allow the panels to move, is a longer-life option for a farmhouse or acreage home.

Farmhouse replacements

A roof replacement on an older farmhouse usually starts with a full tear-off, then a look at the decking, new drip edge at eaves and rakes, and flashing at chimneys and every place an addition meets the original house.

Repairs and storm checks

Many calls are for a roof repair: a few blown-off shingles, a leaking pipe boot or a panel lifted in a storm. If you aren't sure what a storm did, a roof inspection with photos tells you before the next rain does.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a permit to replace a roof in unincorporated north St. Charles County?

St. Charles County lists replacing a roof among projects that generally do not require a building permit. The exception that catches people in north county is the floodplain. If your home or building sits in the county's regulated floodplain, reroofing requires a county floodplain development permit even though no building permit is needed. Check your address against the county floodplain maps or call County Building and Code Enforcement at 636-949-7345.

How do I know if my property is in the regulated floodplain?

St. Charles County regulates development in the floodway, floodway fringe and density floodway shown on the 2016 flood insurance rate maps. Much of the low ground between the Missouri and Mississippi rivers falls in those zones, but it varies parcel by parcel. The county's Floodplain Development Permit page and its building office can tell you whether your address is affected before roofing work starts.

What is the 50 percent substantial damage rule?

If a structure in the floodplain is substantially damaged and the cost of repairing it reaches 50% or more of its pre-damage market value, St. Charles County requires it to be brought into compliance, including a lowest floor at least one foot above base flood elevation. A roof alone rarely triggers it, but a roof that is part of a larger storm or flood repair can count toward that total.

Can you put a new metal roof on my pole barn or shop building?

Yes. Exposed-fastener metal panels are the common choice for pole barns, machine sheds and shops because they cover large areas economically. The screws and their rubber washers do need periodic inspection and replacement over the life of the roof. We look at the purlins and existing panels first and tell you whether new panels can go over sound framing or whether framing repair comes first.

Was there a tornado in north St. Charles County recently?

Yes. The National Weather Service confirmed an EF-0 tornado on November 4 to 5, 2024 with a six-mile track through northwestern St. Charles County. It partly destroyed a pole barn and caused minor roof damage to homes. Damage like lifted shingles or loose metal panels can be easy to miss from the ground, so it is worth having roofs and outbuildings looked at after a storm.

Do you work on old farmhouses in the Orchard Farm area?

Yes. Older farmhouses often have board decking with gaps, past additions with their own rooflines, porch roofs at low pitch and sometimes more than one layer of shingles. Code does not allow a new roof over two or more existing layers, so those become full tear-offs. We photograph the decking and framing so you can see what the house needs before you decide.
